My old website was a template I set up years ago and never really finished. Rebuilding it properly was on my someday list, the kind of project that turns into months of evenings. This week I rebuilt it from scratch with AI. It took hours.

I don't want to oversell that. The interesting part isn't that the AI was fast. It's where the speed came from, where it went wrong, and which decisions it couldn't make for me. That split is the same one I see when I bring AI into exposure science work, so it seemed worth writing down.

## What went fast

Nearly everything I would have dreaded:

- **Rewriting the site, not just moving it.** I didn't want a straight migration. I wanted a new site in a modern framework, with a new structure and design. The AI built that from a conversation about what I wanted, then pulled over the old content and reworked it.
- **The CV.** I gave it my latest CV as a Word file, and it turned that into a web page and a printable version and updated the rest of the site to match.
- **The project pages.** Each tool now has its own page, written from what I told it about the project, with screenshots for public tools and illustrations for client work.
- **The plumbing.** Search engine metadata, redirects from the old addresses, a sitemap, the image people see when a link is shared. None of this is hard, but all of it is tedious, and I would have skipped half of it.

I directed the work and reviewed it. I wrote very little code.

## What it got wrong

Plenty, and that's normal. Some examples:

- **My own job history.** It merged three positions at a previous employer into one. Small, but that's exactly the kind of error that undermines a CV.
- **What counts as AI.** It labeled my climbing recommender an AI tool. It's a recommender system. Calling everything AI is a habit worth resisting, especially if you want people to believe you when it really is AI.
- **A number.** A draft post said our claims work covered systems serving 12 million people, while my CV said 26 million. I gave it the source spreadsheets, and in a few minutes it matched them against EPA's public water system database: about 26 million. The CV was right, the draft was wrong, and I would not have caught it by rereading.
- **AI checking AI.** I had a second AI tool audit the site for search and layout problems. It found a real bug that the first one had introduced. It also flagged two problems that didn't exist, because it was looking at an old version of the page. A reviewer, human or not, needs checking too.

None of this was a reason to stop. It was a reason to read everything.

## What stayed mine

The decisions that mattered most were ones the AI couldn't make, because they depend on things it doesn't know:

- **What goes public.** Most of my work is litigation support. I decided on day one that the site describes chemicals and the kind of work, never clients or cases. The AI followed that rule. It didn't set it.
- **What a reader will take the wrong way.** I had a project page on AI agents that review expert reports. It's a good idea. I took it down anyway, because no lawyer wants to read that a model is reviewing their expert's report.
- **What gives too much away.** A map of our claims work showed two small territories by name. Together with other public information, that was enough to point at a specific client, so I grouped them. Another project page described an idea I'm not ready to share, so it's hidden for now.
- **What I actually want to say.** The AI drafted plenty of copy. Some of it I kept. A lot of it I rewrote, because it was accurate but didn't sound like me.

## The takeaway

The gap between an idea and a working version has collapsed. Things I used to file under someday now take an afternoon. What hasn't changed is that someone has to know the subject, own the judgment calls, and check the output. In my work, that's a scientist, and it always should be.

The same split holds in exposure and risk assessment. AI can pull and screen a literature review, build a data pipeline, or draft a dashboard in a fraction of the time. Someone still has to decide what the question is, what the data can support, and what can be said in public. That's where I spend my time now, and it's a better use of it.
